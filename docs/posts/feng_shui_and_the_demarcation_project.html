Review of the Paper "Feng Shui and the Demarcation Project"

Some time ago, I read the paper Feng Shui and the Demarcation Project, by Damian Fernandez-Beanato, and sent it to my friend Ruan Comelli. I told him that I can separate science from pseudo-science but, even after a Bachelor and a Master's degree, I still struggle to argue and convince people what is and what is not science. I do think I am not alone!

Ruan asked me tell him what I think about the paper, and he suggested me to write a blog post intead of writing to him.

Argument Structure

  1. the demarcation problem and its relevance
  2. social implications of the discussion
  3. problem in science communication to the general public

The Relevance of Demarcating Science

We learn at school that science are the disciplines that apply the scientific method:

  1. make a hypothesis;
  2. design an experiment to test the hypothesis;
  3. carry out the experiment;
  4. analyse the results;
  5. repeat until you are satisfied.

Is it really that simple? Well, no, and as Fernandez-Beanato (2021) says:

Science is a complex phenomenon composed of many epistemically relevant "dimensions", facets or aspects: it includes methodic, propositional, pragmatical, psychological, attitudinal, social, axiological, institutional and other types of epistemically relevant components.

I kinda want to compare with "knowing a person". Some may say that you need a few minutes to understand a person. I don't think so. People are complex, they have history, social and cultural context, feelings and perceptions, and all of that make people who they are. I don't think we can transform all those "dimensions", facets, and aspects of a person to simple terms. Yes, we may do that to analyse specific situations or behaviors, but we will never truly understand a person without it all.

You may be wondering: do we have to know all those "relevant 'dimensions', facets, and aspects" to decide what is and what is not science? The answer of the authors is yes, we do. In that regard, their intention is to provide us a broad list of what makes something a science.

You may think that having a list of 217 properties is awful and long. But, come on, if science is complex, don't you want to be able to see it in all its complexity? Don't you want to see

If you are a methodic maniac like me, you will love that: you take the discipline you want to analyse and go through their list, point by point. You will be delighted to know that their list has 217 properties. That is not all! Most of the them are unnecessary and insufficient to decide whether the discipline is a science or not.

By the end of the 217 items in your analysis, you will realize that things are just getting started. After that step, you have to compare your analysis with those of other "reference" disciplines (known science, pseudo-science, and non-science disciplines). Hopefully, you will realize that the discipline you are analysing either has many characteristics of a science or way too few of them, at which point you will be able to decide whether it is a science or not.

I know, I know, it sounds overwhelming and complicated... Don't worry, it really is! Actually, that is what philosophers and scientists do that all the time (though often not with the full list of 217 items).

Analysis of Feng Shui

The authors did a superb job at arguing and explaining why Feng Shui is not a science (I will not repeat it here). Out of the list of 217 properties, they went through 36, providing nice explanations and clear arguments for whether Feng Shui has it or not. 36 out of 217 does not sound like enough at first. Yet, as I read through their work, it was pretty clear to me (as someone who knowns science) what the conclusion was, and I do not see any need to analyse the remaining properties.

Pseudo-science

Okay, we have a clear and good argument for saying that Feng Shui is not scientific. More specifically, it is a pseudo-science because it tries to appear like a science (as something that has a theoretical model and empirical evidence to support it). Or, as the authors say:

Feng shui has become a pseudo-science in modern times because of its unjustified continuity in the face of much more scientific and available alternatives.

So what the heck should we ordinary people understand by X is a pseudo-science? X is a pseudo-science means we have epistemically better alternatives for X.

(I guess should tell you what epistemically better mean, but I will not).

Social Aspect of Discussing What is Science

Saying that a discipline is or is not a science has an objective component: we want to know what features it has (and how reliable are its results too). Yet, the paper shines for me because the author do not let us forget about its social component. As they put it:

An intuition of interconnectedness and interdependence between humans and their surrounding environments is coherent, rational, reasonable and correct according to current physics, chemistry, biology, ecology, economy and psychology. (...) This, and the abovementioned empirical successes, are reasons why the claim that feng shui is not scientific does not imply that feng shui lacks cultural value or that it cannot be a good alternative in some contexts.

Most often, when I hear people saying "that is not a science", I see them disregarding one's emotions and feelings. People turn to Feng Shui (and other pseudo-science) not necessarily because they agree with "theory", but because they are suffering (in some sense). If we really have better alternatives to Feng Shui, then we must use them to address what matters: relieving one's suffer.

I think we should be open. People are emotional beings. I find people saying things more often because of what they feel and perceive, not what the reality truly is. Using the argument that something is not scientific, though objectively accurate, is often useless for all parties. Don't get me wrong, I advocate for science, rationality, thinking about problems and being objective when solving them. But ultimately our goal is to solve an emotional problem: how people feel, and being purely objective can only get us so far on that.

This, and the abovementioned empirical successes, are reasons why the claim that feng shui is not scientific does not imply that feng shui lacks cultural value or that it cannot be a good alternative in some contexts. But the temporal dimension, the social or cultural context and the availability of epistemically better or worse alternatives should play their parts in assessments of pseudoscientificity

Conclusion

Science is one of the greatest achievements of humanity, and we should use it as a foundation for our decisions. Desires and emotions are vital, and they have their place: they define our desired outcome. Yet, science should be both the "why" (the reason to choose one action instead of the alternatives) and the "how" (the action details that better lead us to the desired outcome) we act towards the desired outcome.

Problem in Communicating Science to the General Public

Main Points

  1. I love how the author decided to show what scientific characteristics Feng Shui has and has not;
  2. The demarcation list has 36 items. I guess it is okay for master to PhD level graduates to read and understand it, maybe some bachelor students get it. However, the general public will understand nothing;
  3. nice distinction between cognitive field, non-science, pseudo-science, and science;
  4. There should be a simpler and smaller list. This guy is clearly an academic that has no real touch with the general public. He tries to address the general public with academic language, which I think is a mistake;
  5. His demarcation list has 217 properties! More that that, there is no consensus or objective way of distinguishing science from pseudo-science with that list!. Yes, I get it: if one considers the full list and all possible combinations, it is hard to give a demarcation, although in practice many cognitive fields either match many or few of them (meaning, in practice it is easy to demarcate).
  6. The author should join scientific communicators to adapt this to a language commom people can understand and talk about.
  7. This reminds me a lot "The Flock of Dodos" (I love the film). Basically, the author contrasts "Intelligent Design" with "The Theory Evolution". Basically, he argues that, yes, evolution is the correct one, but we scientists are doing A VERY POOR WORK ON COMMUNICATION, and that's where things like "intelligent design" (and religion in that matter) capitalize: in communicating clear and easy-to-understand concepts with people that are open, friendly, understanding, and nice.
