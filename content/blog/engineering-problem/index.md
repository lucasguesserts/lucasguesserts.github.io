Today, I felt a sense of pride in my work! not because I delivered a major feature or solved a critical bug, but because I was intentional about my approach. I was mindful of design choices, trade-offs, and prioritization.

I was asked to make a small, specific change in my company’s system. And I did exactly that. But I didn’t stop there. I took the opportunity to study the broader problem that this piece of code was meant to address. I wanted to understand not just what I was changing, but why it was needed and how well the current design was performing.

Digging into the details, I uncovered several flaws in the existing design. As engineers, we know this is common: resources are always limited, and the "perfect" solution is rarely feasible. We constantly balance the ideal with what’s possible, given time, people, and business constraints. In this case, the right call was to implement the requested change and move on. But I documented my findings, knowing these issues might resurface and that being prepared is part of being a responsible engineer.

So why investigate beyond the immediate ask? Curiosity, for one. I want to understand what I’m building and why. But more importantly, I feel responsible for the code I touch. I need to know its limitations and the rationale behind its design. If someone (often myself) asks about a decision, I want to provide a thoughtful answer, not just "I did what I was told". Engineers don’t have the luxury of ignorance; we must be able to explain our choices. After all, we designed it.

## What It Means to Be an Engineer

[Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/engineering) describes **engineering** as:

> the application of science and mathematics by which the properties of matter and the sources of energy in nature are made useful to people

Similarly, the [Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/dictionary/english/engineering) defines is as:

> the study of using scientific principles to design and build machines, structures, and other things, including bridges, roads, vehicles, and buildings

These are generally applicable, but they miss key aspects of what it means to be an engineer in practice. For me, being an engineer means:

1. Solving problems, building things, and making things happen
2. Being a scientist: conducting evidence-based investigations using the best epistemological tools available
3. Uncovering, understanding, and deciding on design trade-offs
4. Doing cool things, because enjoying the work is essential for motivation

## Conclusion

In the end, engineering is about more than just implementing solutions. It iss about asking the right questions and thinking beyond the immediate task. So next time you face a problem, consider these three questions:

1. What is the problem I have? (A question not asked often enough!)
2. What would it take to solve the problem and prevent it from recurring?
3. What can I actually do with the resources I have? What are the trade-offs, and how do I balance them?
